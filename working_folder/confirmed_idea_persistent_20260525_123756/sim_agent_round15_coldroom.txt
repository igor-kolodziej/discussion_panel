**Control Point Tier:** Tier 3: operational control, not durable market control.

Cold-Room Controller Emergency Bank has real urgency: spoilage risk creates immediate willingness to pay, and obsolete exact-SKU availability plus same-day dispatch could produce fast cash. But it is still mostly a stocked-parts/reseller model unless the founder controls routed demand from installers or a uniquely indexed compatibility archive. The unit economics are plausible at small scale, but owner earnings are not yet proven because ticket sizes may be modest, emergency frequency may be sporadic, and the first 5 dispatches require live failure timing.

**Strongest Objections/Caveats**

- Demand may be urgent but too fragmented: restaurants, florists, small grocery, and cold rooms use varied controllers, probes, wiring setups, and installer preferences.
- 30 stocked controllers/probes may not cover enough failure patterns to create dependable conversion.
- Willingness to pay exists during spoilage risk, but many buyers will call their existing refrigeration installer first, not search for an unknown emergency bank.
- Owner earnings are unclear: high gross margin on parts may be offset by sourcing, testing, compatibility support, returns, courier urgency, and low absolute order value.
- Retention is weaker than PLC/HMI or contract-book ideas unless installers repeatedly route failures.

**Strongest Copy Risk**

Refrigeration wholesalers and installers can copy the practical offer by stocking common obsolete controllers/probes, buying contractor stock, and offering emergency courier/van dispatch. The current defense is only “narrow obsolete SKU archive + urgent dispatch,” which is not enough to exceed the copy cap. This triggers the strict cap below 85.
