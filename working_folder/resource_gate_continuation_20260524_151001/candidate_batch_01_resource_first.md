# Candidate Batch 01 - Resource-First Search

Generated: 2026-05-24.

Purpose: produce harsh, non-confirmed candidates that start from a scarce asset or unavoidable transaction gate. Internal scores are guesses, not simulated Zero To One scores.

## Live Candidates

### 1. Orphan Dental Implant Parts Bank

- **Buyer:** 50-60 year-old working adults with a broken crown, abutment, screw, or tool-dependent repair on an older implant system. Dentist is the installer, but the patient is the economic buyer because the alternative is a costly replacement plan.
- **Trigger:** a visible tooth or implant restoration fails, the implant body is still salvageable, and the needed component is discontinued, non-mainstream, or unavailable through normal dental distributors.
- **Control point:** owned, traceable, sealed new-old-stock inventory of compatible screws, abutments, drivers, torque adapters, scan bodies, analogs, and small tools for named legacy implant systems, plus paid search/reserve deposits tied to exact implant model IDs.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** buy small sealed lots from retiring dentists, prosthetic labs, dealer liquidations, and EU auction listings; build a compatibility matrix for 10-15 high-pain legacy systems; get 5-10 dentists to agree to use patient-provided traceable parts when clinically appropriate; collect 10+ refundable patient/dentist search deposits for named missing parts.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** implant OEMs and distributors prefer new systems and do not want liability for obsolete lines; dentists usually monetize replacement or larger treatment plans; general dental labs do not want to warehouse many low-volume, traceability-sensitive SKUs. A copycat cannot instantly buy the same fragmented named lots once the founder owns them.
- **Economics:** purchase small parts for roughly 20-500 PLN each when found in lots; sell urgent parts/searches for 600-2,500 PLN, with emergency premium for avoiding implant removal, new surgery, temporary restorations, and months of treatment. Search deposits improve cash conversion.
- **Fatal risk:** medical-device traceability, counterfeit risk, and dentist willingness. This only works if the business sells traceable sealed parts and never asks dentists to implant unverifiable used parts.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 88. Strongest resource-first candidate, but not above 90 until legal/traceability and dentist adoption are proven.

### 2. Work-Age Hearing Aid Salvage And Parts Bank

- **Buyer:** 50-60 year-old workers who depend on a specific hearing aid model for meetings, sales, teaching, driving, or factory work, and cannot wait weeks for official replacement.
- **Trigger:** one device fails, a receiver/wire/battery door/microphone cover/casing part is no longer stocked, and the clinic proposes a full new pair.
- **Control point:** owned inventory of donor units and new-old-stock model-specific receivers, battery doors, chargers, earmold connectors, cables, and shells, mapped by exact model and serial-compatible generation.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** buy 100-200 donor/new-old-stock items from Allegro, OLX, eBay, estate sales, and closing audiology shops; create 30 landing pages for exact model-part searches; get 20 paid diagnostic/search deposits; contract one repair technician for installation where legally required.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** audiology clinics and OEMs are economically biased toward selling new aids; stocking obsolete tiny parts creates inventory and repair liability with lower revenue than replacement. A broad hearing chain can copy only if it chooses to undermine its own upgrade funnel.
- **Economics:** donor units at 50-400 PLN can yield one or more 300-1,500 PLN repairs/parts; high urgency and search-intent CAC. Repeat purchase is weak, but the SEO inventory map and model coverage compound.
- **Fatal risk:** could be regulated repair rather than simple part resale; quality/reliability complaints can destroy trust; some models require proprietary programming.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 84. Good economics, but weaker than dental because parts may be easier to source and less painful than implant failure.

### 3. Genetic Biobank Emergency Spares Bank

- **Buyer:** small hospital genetics labs, academic biobanks, IVF/genetics labs, and private molecular diagnostic labs holding frozen DNA, tissue, cell, plasma, or embryo-adjacent research samples.
- **Trigger:** an ultra-low freezer, LN2 storage component, barcode/rack system, controller, gasket, tray, canister, or sample-format accessory fails and the lab needs the exact compatible part before samples warm, are mishandled, or become unusable.
- **Control point:** owned spare inventory for old but common freezer/LN2/rack systems, plus pre-negotiated rights to buy shutdown-lab surplus and emergency retainers for named equipment models.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** identify 20 common models in Polish/EU genetics labs; buy high-failure spare lots from lab liquidations and surplus dealers; sign 3 emergency-retainer LOIs or paid retainers from labs; document one real emergency delivery or mock emergency with timed courier feasibility.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** OEMs prefer selling new freezers and do not carry long-tail obsolete parts; generic service firms carry generic components but not odd sample-format racks and model-specific parts; labs hate holding dead inventory for every possible failure.
- **Economics:** annual retainers of 2,000-8,000 PLN per lab plus high-margin part resale/rental; one prevented sample-loss event is worth far more than the fee. Cash conversion can be good if retainers fund inventory.
- **Fatal risk:** labs may already have service contracts and may distrust an unknown founder in an emergency. Need narrow parts where official contracts demonstrably fail.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 85. Better than a service desk because inventory is real, but trust and incumbent service contracts cap it unless a specific unsupported model niche is found.

### 4. Legacy EEG/PSG Cable And Sensor Bank

- **Buyer:** private sleep labs, neurology clinics, hospital EEG rooms, and research labs using older but functioning EEG/PSG systems.
- **Trigger:** a patient cable, amplifier adapter, electrode harness, headbox cable, oximetry lead, respiratory belt connector, or reference sensor fails before a scheduled reimbursed study or grant-critical data collection window.
- **Control point:** owned inventory of exact legacy cables/adapters/sensors for named EEG/PSG platforms, with overnight rental/sale deposits and compatibility evidence by device generation.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** buy 30-50 named legacy accessories from EU/US surplus channels and retiring labs; create compatibility sheets; call 100 sleep/EEG labs and secure 5 paid standby deposits or paid rentals; document average OEM lead time for missing parts.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** OEMs prefer new systems; distributors avoid discontinued accessories; clinical labs are too fragmented to hold each other's spares. A biomedical service firm can copy some of it, but not instantly if inventory lots are already owned.
- **Economics:** buy accessories for 100-1,000 PLN and rent/sell for 600-4,000 PLN depending on urgency; paid standby plans can improve retention.
- **Fatal risk:** too close to generic device-service rescue if the parts are easy to obtain; scheduled tests can often be postponed; copy risk from biomedical service companies.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 82. Needs proof of truly unavailable named parts and non-postponable buyers to break out of the old failed pattern.

### 5. Orphan Cardiology Biosignal Lead Bank

- **Buyer:** small cardiology practices, occupational medicine providers, private hospitals, and ambulatory Holter/ECG labs using older Holter, stress-test, ABPM, or event-recorder systems.
- **Trigger:** a patient lead set, charger, battery pack, recorder cradle, belt, or proprietary connector fails and blocks paid tests, driver/work clearance exams, or scheduled monitoring.
- **Control point:** owned inventory of legacy recorder accessories for named systems, with deposits from clinics for specific model coverage.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** buy end-of-life accessories from retiring clinics and surplus listings; map the top 20 systems used by Polish private cardiology labs; secure 5 paid standby deposits from labs that cannot get OEM parts within 7 days.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** manufacturers prefer device replacement; distributors do not want obsolete medical accessory inventory; clinics avoid holding spare stock across many legacy systems.
- **Economics:** parts bought for 50-800 PLN can rent/sell for 500-3,000 PLN; clinics pay because one blocked test day may exceed the part cost.
- **Fatal risk:** cardiology practices may have more common substitute devices than EEG labs; if generic leads work, the control point collapses.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 79. Viable only if a hidden list of proprietary, unavailable, high-frequency failures exists.

### 6. Discontinued Neurostimulator Charger And Remote Bank

- **Buyer:** 50-60 year-old patients with implanted spinal cord stimulators, DBS-adjacent devices, or other neurostimulation systems who lose or break the external charger/controller.
- **Trigger:** therapy stops, pain returns, or device cannot be managed while official replacement is delayed, expensive, or routed through a slow clinic/OEM process.
- **Control point:** owned inventory of compatible external chargers/controllers/remotes for named models, reserved by paid deposits and delivered through licensed clinical channels where required.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** verify pairing rules for 5 major model families; buy only legally transferable unused or unpaired external accessories; obtain 10 patient search deposits and 2 clinician opinions on transferability.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** OEMs prefer controlled replacement channels; clinics do not want spare-device inventory; used medical-device markets are fragmented and risky.
- **Economics:** high willingness to pay if the external unit is the bottleneck; gross margins could be strong because donor/surplus units are distressed inventory.
- **Fatal risk:** likely pairing, warranty, safety, and legal restrictions. If units cannot be transferred or re-paired, the business dies.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 70. Interesting asset, but too likely to fail the 2-month controllability test.

### 7. Polish Founder-Mutation Positive-Control Kit Bank

- **Buyer:** molecular diagnostics labs validating or maintaining tests for locally common hereditary cancer, thrombophilia, pharmacogenetic, and rare-disease variants.
- **Trigger:** IVDR/ISO validation or assay launch requires positive controls for variants that are not conveniently available in global control catalogs.
- **Control point:** a stocked catalog of synthetic/reference positive-control materials for Polish/CEE founder variants, bundled with lot documentation and validation-use workflows.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** interview 20 labs; identify 10 variants missing from commercial catalogs; commission synthetic controls or plasmid constructs; pre-sell 5 kits to labs.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** global control vendors under-serve small local founder-variant demand; local labs know the pain but do not commercialize it.
- **Economics:** controls can have high gross margins if kits are reusable across labs; sales are B2B and repeatable when assays update.
- **Fatal risk:** copyable by one specialist vendor contract; the founder's control point is not strong unless it owns patient-derived or otherwise exclusive materials, which is hard to secure legally in 2 months.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 76. Kill or radically reshape unless an exclusive material/right can be secured.

### 8. Sealed Diabetes Pump Supply Transition Bank

- **Buyer:** 50-60 insulin-dependent patients using older pumps whose infusion sets, reservoirs, chargers, clips, or reader accessories are disappearing before reimbursement/upgrade timing.
- **Trigger:** patient is forced into a gap between old-device supply and new-device approval/reimbursement.
- **Control point:** sealed, in-date supply lots and accessory inventory for named pump generations, reserved with patient deposits.
- **2-month proof under 100k PLN:** buy only in-date sealed accessories from legitimate liquidation sources; create exact-model search pages; collect deposits for named shortages.
- **Why incumbents structurally avoid/cannot copy:** manufacturers prefer migration to new pumps; pharmacies and distributors avoid near-end-of-life stock.
- **Economics:** urgency is high, but resale margins may be capped by regulated distribution and expiry risk.
- **Fatal risk:** medical consumable resale regulation, expiry, counterfeit risk, and patient-safety liability. Also too easy for a manufacturer or distributor to solve if it wants to.
- **Internal 0-100 guess:** 64. Kill unless regulation unexpectedly allows a safe, traceable non-consumable accessory-only niche.
