# Strict Simulated Zero To One Reviews: F4-F6

## F4. RGP/Scleral Lens Continuity Rights

### Score

**79 / 100**

### Diagnosis

The patient pain is real: a 50-60-year-old dependent on specialty RGP or scleral lenses may lose work, driving, or daily function when a lens breaks and refit capacity is slow. The control point is weaker than it first appears, because the scarce asset is not only a trial set or design library; it is licensed specialty optometry skill, clinical trust, and the ability to safely remake or refit. If the founder cannot control clinical execution, the business becomes a boutique optometry coordination service with inventory attached.

### Control Point Tier

**Tier C+: partially ownable asset, clinician-dependent.**

Physical trial sets and design libraries can be bought or optioned, but they are not sufficient control points unless paired with contracted specialist optometrists and rights to remake specific legacy designs.

### Strongest Copy Risk

A specialty optometry clinic or contact-lens lab can copy the continuity offer through one retiring-lab acquisition or one senior optometrist hire. Large chains may ignore the niche, but small specialists already have trust.

### Main Objections

- Clinical labor and trust may dominate inventory rights.
- Patient acquisition is narrow and likely referral-driven.
- Repeat revenue is low unless the company becomes a full continuity-care provider.
- A 2-month POC can prove deposits, but not dominance.

### Profitability / CAC / Payback View

Margins can be good on remakes and emergency refits, but CAC is uncertain because patients are hard to target directly and optometrists may not route cases to an unknown intermediary. Payback can be acceptable only if deposits are collected through existing clinicians.

### Best Move

**Kill for this run.** It is useful, but not threshold-grade under the requested control-point discipline.

## F5. Genetic Biobank Emergency Spares Bank

### Score

**83 / 100**

### Diagnosis

This is a real B2B urgency idea with potentially good cash conversion: a lab holding irreplaceable DNA, cell, tissue, or plasma samples cannot calmly wait when a storage-system failure threatens continuity. The control point is owned emergency inventory plus surplus-shutdown rights, which is more concrete than routing or consulting. The cap is that official service contracts, freezer vendors, and lab-equipment distributors already own much of the trust layer, and they can copy common spares if demand is proven. The founder must prove that the valuable inventory is not commodity freezer parts but weird model-specific racks, controllers, canisters, trays, barcode formats, and compatibility knowledge that incumbents do not want to stock.

### Control Point Tier

**Tier B-: ownable emergency inventory, weak exclusivity unless model-specific.**

The asset is concrete, but not automatically exclusive. It becomes stronger only when the company options shutdown-lab surplus and maps obscure compatibility across legacy storage systems.

### Strongest Copy Risk

A freezer-service company, biobank equipment distributor, or OEM service partner can add a spare-parts emergency shelf and call existing customers. They may not want the long tail, but they can cover the highest-volume parts quickly.

### Main Objections

- Labs may rely on vendor service contracts and not trust an unknown founder during a crisis.
- The most urgent failures may require licensed technicians, not shipped parts.
- Holding inventory can consume cash before demand is proven.
- Emergency retainers are attractive, but a solo founder may struggle to guarantee response.

### Profitability / CAC / Payback View

The best model is retainer plus emergency margin, not pure resale. CAC is reachable through direct lab-manager outreach, university/IVF/genetics lab lists, and biobank networks, but trust-building may be slow. Payback can be proven in 2 months only through paid retainers and an emergency courier drill, not LOIs.

### Best Move

**Narrow/research further.** It might be a good cash business, but it does not clear the `>90` simulated threshold unless the founder finds a specific obsolete storage-system family where spare scarcity is documented and buyers prepay retainers.

## F6. Genotype-Clear Dog Germplasm Option Pool

### Score

**86 / 100**

### Diagnosis

This has the strongest pure control-point logic among F4-F6: named frozen semen lots are unique, exhaustible, optionable assets, and genotype documentation can convert private, politically traded material into a cleaner reservation-backed transaction. The buyer pain is not abstract animal health; it is the breeder's need to preserve desirable bloodlines while avoiding hidden carrier risk, failed litters, import friction, and reputation damage. The economics can work because a single litter can justify a high stud fee or reservation premium. The score remains below threshold because the current version is too broad and too trust-heavy: breed politics, storage-clinic cooperation, kennel reputation, semen quality, and genetic-test interpretation all sit outside the founder's natural authority.

### Control Point Tier

**Tier B+: named physical/reproductive asset option control.**

The exact lots are unique and cannot be copied once optioned. The tier is not A yet because commercial value depends on verified semen quality, ownership authority, pedigree status, health/genotype tests, and breeder acceptance.

### Strongest Copy Risk

Established breeders, kennel clubs, reproduction vets, or existing semen banks can run the same transaction inside their trust network. They cannot copy optioned named lots, but they can copy the playbook and have better social access unless the founder starts in one breed and locks assets before announcing the category.

### Main Objections

- Trust and politics may dominate the transaction more than data quality.
- The market may be too small if the first breed is chosen badly.
- Genetics can expose bad news; some owners may prefer opacity over documentation.
- Veterinary reproduction logistics and semen-quality proof require specialist help.
- The idea can become a niche brokerage unless it controls exclusive lots and deposits, not listings.

### Profitability / CAC / Payback View

Gross margins can be high if option fees are small and buyer deposits/reservation premiums are collected before deep spend. CAC is tolerable only inside one breed community, through direct outreach and a contracted insider/reproduction-vet advisor, not broad ads. Payback can be fast if the POC secures 10-20 optioned lots and 3-5 breeder deposits. Retention is moderate: serious breeders make repeated breeding decisions, but each lot is a finite asset, so compounding depends on becoming the trusted archive for one breed's genotype-cleared legacy semen.

### Best Move

**Pivot and sharpen.** The promising version is not a general dog semen marketplace. It is a one-breed, genotype-cleared frozen-semen rights bank for a high-WTP breed with known inherited-disease anxiety, concentrated private semen lots, imported-male dependence, and enough litter economics to justify deposits. It should be re-scored only after specifying the breed, exact genotype panel, storage/quality proof, option contract, deposit flow, and local insider contractor.
