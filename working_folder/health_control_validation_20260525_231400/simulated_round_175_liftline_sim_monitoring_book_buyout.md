## Chosen Candidate

Idea name: LiftLine Emergency-Phone SIM Monitoring Book Buyout

One-sentence thesis: Buy small retiring lift/intercom installers' recurring books of elevator emergency-phone GSM SIM connectivity and automatic test-call monitoring, transferring customer-approved billing and device administration before close.

Exact buyer/control source: The economic "seller" is a retiring or distracted lift/intercom installer with 100-1,000 active lift emergency-phone SIMs and test-call logs for apartment buildings, small commercial sites, schools, clinics, and property managers. The paying customers are building owners/property managers who approve billing transfer.

Acute trigger: The seller wants to retire, exit low-margin admin, or stop managing telecom renewals; customers need uninterrupted emergency-phone connectivity and test evidence but do not want to renegotiate with lift maintainers, telecoms, and alarm centers.

Control point: Customer-approved payment direction, transferred SIM/MVNO account or reseller contract, ICCID/device/lift-location register, test-call log history, failure-notification workflow, phone/domain/inbox handoff, seller introduction, and first-month collected recurring cash before acquisition close.

60-day proof: Sign one seller option covering at least 150 active SIM/test-log accounts, obtain customer-approved billing transfer on 60+ accounts, transfer telecom/admin credentials, complete one monthly test-log cycle, collect first-month recurring cash, and pay seller only from transferred accounts.

6-month POC: Acquire or option 400-800 active SIM/test-log accounts across 2-4 sellers, collect 35,000-90,000 PLN monthly recurring revenue, maintain churn below 8%, automate monthly test evidence/failure notifications, and add a second MVNO for redundancy.

Economics: Charge 35-90 PLN per lift/SIM/month depending on SIM, test-log, alerting, and reporting scope. Gross margin 45-70% after telecom, call-test platform, and support costs. Seller paid 4-8x transferred monthly gross profit, with earnout based on retained accounts. Customer acquisition cost is embedded in seller handoff and billing transfer.

Copy risk: Lift maintainers, intercom installers, telecom resellers, alarm centers, and property-management software vendors can copy. The wedge is not inventing monitoring; it is buying already-paying books before they fragment, with customer-approved payment transfer and continuity as the reason customers stay.

Why incumbents cannot copy before the founder controls the specific asset/account/case/claim/lot/payment stream: Once customers approve billing transfer and SIM/device administration moves, the founder controls the recurring payment stream, ICCID/device register, monthly test cycle, and customer contact trail. Competitors can sell new accounts but cannot copy the transferred cash book.

Why it is not a service, report, app, dashboard, database, marketplace, or generic broker: The asset is a transferred recurring payment book with SIM accounts, device register, monthly test logs, billing direction, and first-month cash. The startup operates the book; it does not sell advice, a report, a marketplace, or an introduction.

Boundaries and legal/provider limits: Licensed lift maintainers remain responsible for lift maintenance, rescue, inspections, and safety defects. Emergency answering/rescue is handled by the existing alarm center or property manager's chosen provider. The startup handles telecom connectivity, automatic test logs, admin, billing, and failure notifications only. It does not certify lift safety or perform lift maintenance.

Duplicate risk: Not the failed legacy industrial spares retainer: this is not emergency repair or technician trust. It is a customer-approved recurring payment book with telecom/admin control and first-month cash before close. Not HeritageDoor, DORA, REDBlocked, TraceFaktura, PromoLeak, CBAM, or Data Act.

Strongest anticipated objections: Safety responsibility may leak despite boundaries; lift firms may bundle this already; property managers may prefer one throat to choke; books may be too small or messy; telecom margin may be thin; customer approvals may be slow; churn after seller handoff may be high; the business may look like a small boring roll-up rather than a venture-scale company.
