# SprayPilot

SprayPilot is a real-production augmentation system for small, high-mix industrial coating shops: it links how a skilled painter moves a spray gun to the film-thickness and defect outcome on the actual part, then gives private haptic guidance that reduces rework and transfers expert technique without requiring a robot.

The first buyer is the owner or production/quality manager of a 10–60-person Polish wet-paint shop that still coats changing metal geometries manually, has at least one repeated part family, already measures dry-film thickness or defects, and plausibly loses more than 12,000 PLN monthly to rework, material variation, or dependence on one expert painter. The urgent event is a recurring part family with unacceptable variation or the need to onboard a second operator. Automotive-scale robotic lines, artistic work, and shops without reliable outcome labels are excluded.

The existing alternatives do not close the same loop. VR products such as SimSpray, VR Paint, and E-CO Painter train generic motion away from production. A robot repeats known geometry but is often uneconomic for a small shop's changing parts. Ordinary quality control finds the defect after coating. SprayPilot learns from the buyer's real part, gun, coating, booth, and measured outcome, then intervenes before the same motion error produces another bad piece.

V1 is a paid shadow-mode experiment, not a finished wearable. A passive visual marker or non-electrical fixture on the gun is observed from approved equipment outside the hazardous zone; no uncertified powered electronics enter an explosive atmosphere. Motion features—speed, angle, orientation, distance proxy, and overlap—are joined to the shop's normal thickness/defect records for 20–30 pieces in one part family. If those features do not explain a decision-relevant share of outcome variation, the product is killed before haptic hardware. If they do, the next paid phase adds an appropriately reviewed/certified wrist or external cue and tests whether feedback improves first-pass yield. The system never controls the gun, ventilation, or a safety function.

The commercial hypothesis is a 10,000–18,000 PLN paid shadow pilot with 40% deposit, followed by a 12,000–22,000 PLN deployment and 4,500–7,000 PLN per booth per month for recipes, onboarding, model updates, and outcome tracking. Early pilot contribution margin is expected at 50–65%; mature blended gross margin is expected at 66–73% after calibration becomes template-driven. A customer continues only if documented avoided rework, material, or onboarding cost is at least 1.5 times the recurring fee in the pilot and two times by month six. No willingness-to-pay claim is treated as evidence until a buyer pays.

The first ten customers are acquired without a partnership. Public industrial directories, coating trade-fair/exhibitor lists, job posts, and supplier case studies can produce a named list of 120 Polish shops. The founder qualifies only owner-led shops with a repeated manual process and existing objective quality records, demonstrates the shadow system, and sells one bounded part-family pilot. Six initial customers are expected from direct founder-led outbound and four from measured-result referrals. Customers 11–100 come from anonymized technique-to-outcome benchmarks, technical search, referrals inside dense subcontracting clusters, and later a hired coating applications engineer. A supplier/reseller channel is optional only after direct unit economics are proven and is not needed for the first year.

The directly controlled asset is not the IMU, camera, marker, or haptic motor. It is the owned calibration and edge software, part/coating technique features, haptic vocabulary, and the growing contractual dataset linking real booth conditions and operator motion to thickness/defect outcomes over time. Raw identifiable worker records and shop process secrets stay customer-owned. Contracts preserve the company's generic model, calibration, and non-identifying feature/outcome derivatives. Within twelve months, control-point proof is three independent paying shops, two part families, at least one measurable outcome improvement, reusable calibration across two deployments, and signed derivative rights. If deployment ten still needs more than 30 custom engineering hours or cross-shop calibration does not improve, the business is a service and is stopped.

The initial moat is narrow but can compound. Every paid part family expands the failure taxonomy, reduces calibration time, improves haptic recipes, and creates an installed history that is costly to replace. VR-training firms do not own real production outcomes; generic sensor vendors do not own the shop-specific and cross-shop mappings; robot vendors are not economical for the beachhead. The strongest copy risk is a coating-equipment or training incumbent adding sensors and using existing distribution. SprayPilot survives only if its accumulated outcome corpus and deployment speed improve faster than that incumbent can move inward. It is non-viable if the result can be reproduced through one sensor purchase and routine process engineering.

The beachhead is small enough to specialize in one manual wet-coating task and large enough to expand across Polish and CEE subcontractors. Expansion is sequential and customer-funded: additional wet coatings and geometries, onboarding and recipe transfer across sites, then adjacent skilled tool paths such as sealant, sanding, or finishing. Longer-term, the outcome-labeled skilled-motor corpus and real-time HCI stack are useful for robot demonstration learning and the future lab. Poland offers lower-cost on-site iteration and dense manufacturing access; it is an entry advantage, not a legal moat.

Founder fit is strong in time-series data, ML, human-signal interpretation, analytics, and AI-assisted software. The missing coating and hazardous-area knowledge is purchased through a fixed-scope coating applications specialist and safety review; the first technical stack uses approved equipment outside the hazardous zone. The founder remains employed and works no more than about five hours daily. Employment ends only after at least five independent paying shops or contracted recurring booths, positive contribution margin on three pilots, at least two paid subscription conversions, a repeatable non-personal acquisition signal, safe repeatable installation, and twelve months of personal runway.

Founder wealth scenarios are commercial hypotheses and do not double-count retained earnings. Business values are equity values after debt; founder cash is only actual cash distributed. While employed, conservative and expected cases reinvest 95% of after-tax cash otherwise available for distribution and cap optional distributions at 5%; the strong case reinvests 100% until scale.

- Conservative, year 8: 120 subscribed booths at 5,500 PLN per month, 25 new deployments at 16,000 PLN, and 20 pilots at 13,000 PLN. Revenue is 8.58m PLN, gross margin 67%, operating margin 26%, and EBIT 2.23m PLN. With 85% founder ownership, 250,000 PLN debt, a 5× EBIT equity value, and 1.0m PLN of actual post-transition founder distributions, estimated founder net worth is about 10.3m PLN. This barely reaches the target and fails if delivery stays bespoke.
- Expected success, year 7: 300 subscribed booths at 6,500 PLN per month, 60 deployments at 18,000 PLN, and 35 pilots at 15,000 PLN. Revenue is 25.0m PLN, gross margin 71%, operating margin 34%, and EBIT 8.5m PLN. With 75% founder ownership after 3m PLN cumulative growth capital, less than 700,000 PLN debt, a 5.5× EBIT equity value, and 2.5m PLN actual post-transition distributions, estimated founder net worth is about 37.1m PLN. Milestones are three shops in year one, 25 recurring booths in year three, and CEE/DACH entry in year four.
- Strong success, year 7: 1,000 subscribed booths at 8,000 PLN per month plus 12m PLN of deployments/data licensing. Revenue is 108m PLN, gross margin 75%, operating margin 40%, and EBIT 43.2m PLN. After 42% dilution and 4m PLN net debt, a 6× EBIT equity value leaves the founder about 148m PLN; no distributions are added.

The expected and conservative cases support the 10m PLN founder-wealth path; strong success is not needed. The conservative case is intentionally thin and makes productized deployment and recurring value non-negotiable.

The 180-day paid-proof path is staged. Days 1–30: spend at most 5,000 PLN on safety/method review and an outside-zone tracking bench. Days 31–75: qualify 15 shops from a 60-account first batch and offer one 10,000–15,000 PLN shadow pilot with 40% deposit. Days 76–135: record 20–30 normal pieces, using the shop's existing quality process, and test whether motion predicts outcome. Days 136–180: deliver the result and sell a second paid intervention/deployment phase only if the data supports it. Maximum pre-proof founder risk is 15,000 PLN. Further spending up to 40,000 PLN requires a deposit and approved method; up to 75,000 PLN requires successful delivery, positive contribution, and a second independent payer; up to 100,000 PLN requires three payers plus recurring demand or a documented viable acquisition channel. Possible family capital is excluded until repeatable paid evidence.

Kill criteria are: no economical approved outside-zone/shadow method; no deposit by day 180; fewer than three serious trials from 60 qualified shops; motion features fail to predict a decision-relevant outcome on two part families; measured value is below 1.5 times the recurring fee; workers reject private feedback; two otherwise willing buyers reject generic derivative rights; the tenth deployment still needs over 30 custom engineering hours; or mature blended gross margin projects below 65%.

If the coating wedge fails, the owned tracking/calibration software, haptic vocabulary, industrial deployment knowledge, and outcome-labeled skilled-movement corpus can transfer to sealant, sanding, assembly, robot demonstration capture, or the future human-interface lab. Passing research or chatbot review would mean only that the idea is strong enough to pursue through staged paid experiments; it would not authorize serious capital spending.
