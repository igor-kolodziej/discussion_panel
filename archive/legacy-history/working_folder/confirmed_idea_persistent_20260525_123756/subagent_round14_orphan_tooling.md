Copy risk: **Medium**
This is not defensible as an abstract idea, but it becomes defensible asset by asset. A signed option on specific molds, fixtures, CAM files, drawings, reorder history, phone/domain, and owner handoff creates real control. The copy risk is mostly before rights are secured: toolshops, liquidators, incumbent suppliers, or a sharp CNC/plastics operator could imitate the process locally.

Objections:
- Tool ownership ambiguity can kill deals unless title/usage rights are documented tool by tool.
- Some “orphan” buyers may have moved on, redesigned parts, or no longer need repeat runs.
- Reactivation sales may be slower than expected if maintenance/procurement teams are fragmented.
- Storage, transport, inspection, insurance, and repair costs can quietly eat margins.
- Incumbent suppliers can attack the best accounts once demand is visible.
- This is operationally messy; the founder must be good at legal paperwork, seller trust, buyer calls, and production routing.

Economics:
Credible enough to clear the cap. The avoided replacement cost of **10k-100k PLN** gives room for a recovery/setup fee, routing margin, and storage/maintenance fee. CAC can be low if acquisition comes from retiring shops, liquidation notices, and direct buyer reactivation, but the real cost is founder time. Payback is plausible after one or two successful orders if the founder avoids buying dead inventory outright and uses options first.

Verdict: **Advance**

This has a real control wedge: orphaned production assets plus customer reorder history. The 60-day proof is concrete: secure options on **10-30 tools**, confirm written title/usage rights, reactivate **2 buyers**, and produce **2 paid orders**. If that proof fails, the idea drops hard; if it works, this can become a defensible local micro-monopoly roll-up rather than a generic tooling broker.
