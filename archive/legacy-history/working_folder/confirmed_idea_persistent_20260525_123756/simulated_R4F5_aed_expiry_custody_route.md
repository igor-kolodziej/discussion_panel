# Simulated Zero To One-Style Scoring: R4F5 AED Pad/Battery Expiry Custody Route

Date: 2026-05-25 Europe/Warsaw.

## Exact Idea Scored

AED Pad/Battery Expiry Custody Route. Acquire or build a route book of small facilities with installed AEDs whose pads/batteries expire, then sell replacement custody, expiry monitoring, and evidence logs. The acquired asset is a registered AED service book, not a generic reminder app.

Buyer: offices, hotels, gyms, schools, warehouses, clinics, and local public facilities with AEDs but no reliable expiry owner. Acute trigger: pads/batteries expire, AED inspection finds non-readiness, insurer/safety auditor asks for evidence, or a facility realizes an AED may fail in an emergency. Control point: AED location registry, installed QR/service stickers, replacement stock, annual custody mandate, and phone routing. Two-month proof: sign/acquire 100 AED locations; complete 20 paid pad/battery replacements; collect annual custody prepayments. Primary caveat: if no route book is acquired, this collapses into reminders and parts resale.

## Score

**83/100**

## Control-Point Tier

**Tier B / Tier 2: installed-base record plus replacement timing custody, but weak route lock unless an existing AED service book is actually acquired.**

The control point can be real if the founder acquires a service book with device serials, model-specific pad/battery expiry dates, site contacts, service stickers, active invoices, phone/domain routing, and seller handoff. Without that, the business is a reminder workflow and commodity consumables resale.

## Strongest Copy Risk

AED distributors, first-aid/safety suppliers, facility-management companies, fire-safety inspectors, or existing AED service firms can add expiry reminders and replacement bundles through their current customer lists. This is especially copyable if the startup does not buy a routed book or install stickers/QR custody on physical devices.

## Strongest Objections And Caveats

- The two-month proof path is plausible only if a real AED reseller/service book is acquired or optioned. Building 100 locations from scratch is too slow for an unknown founder.
- Replacement cadence is annual or multi-year, so retention exists but transaction frequency is weaker than fiscal devices, route consumables, or industrial MRO.
- Buyers may see pads/batteries as low-stakes procurement unless an audit, incident, insurance requirement, or public-access AED registry obligation creates urgency.
- Device-model fragmentation can create stock mistakes and returns.
- Public facilities may involve procurement friction and slower payments.
- Gross margin is credible on replacement parts, but owner earnings depend on route density and prepayments, not the monitoring layer.

## Economics View

Replacement orders of roughly 150-800 PLN with 30-55% gross margin are plausible depending on device model and sourcing. Annual custody prepayments can improve cash conversion, but the business needs a high active-device count to produce meaningful owner earnings because pads and batteries do not turn frequently. CAC is acceptable only when inherited from an acquired service book or from inspection/safety channels already touching the AED. Cold outbound to facilities would likely be too slow and low-conviction.

## Verdict

**Hold / pivot, not real-gate eligible.**

This does not clear the simulated `>87` gate. To become competitive, it would need a signed option over a live AED service book, not a plan to build one; a mandatory or quasi-mandatory inspection trigger; and prepaid replacement/custody mandates from enough sites to prove that this is a routed transaction flow rather than a reminder app plus spare parts.
