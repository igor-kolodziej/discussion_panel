Score this business idea 0-100 and include strongest objections.

Founder context: unknown Warsaw solo founder, contractors allowed, max 100,000 PLN, 6-month POC, part-time.

Idea name:
EEvidence Addressee Appointment Release Desk

One-sentence thesis:
A deadline-driven appointment-execution desk for non-EU and cross-border digital service providers whose EU enterprise customers, procurement teams, counsel, security reviewers, or launch owners are blocking a renewal, procurement, vendor approval, or EU market launch until the provider can prove an EU e-Evidence designated establishment or legal-representative route and a practical order-intake process before the August 2026 application date.

Exact buyer:
Small and mid-market SaaS, hosting, cloud, collaboration, messaging, email, domain/DNS, marketplace, developer-platform, community-platform, file-storage, customer-support, authentication, CPaaS, and managed-service providers that offer services in the EU but lack a mature EU legal/compliance operations team. The first buyer is a provider with a named EU customer, reseller, procurement process, counsel review, security questionnaire, DPA review, or EU launch checklist asking how the provider will receive, route, and evidence compliance with EU e-Evidence designation obligations. Exclude consumer mega-platforms, telecom carriers, providers already handled by large EU counsel, encrypted content disputes, criminal-law advice, and any case where the buyer expects the startup to receive or decide law-enforcement orders.

Acute trigger:
An EU enterprise customer, reseller, procurement team, DPO, external counsel, security reviewer, investor, acquirer, or launch owner asks for proof that the provider has designated an EU establishment or appointed a legal representative/addressee for European Production Orders and Preservation Orders, and that internal intake, escalation, data-location, logging, conflict-check, and authority-handling roles are defined. The commercial event is a live renewal, procurement approval, vendor onboarding, due-diligence request, EU launch, reseller agreement, or customer security/privacy review, not abstract regulatory education.

Control point:
Signed appointment-release mandate over the specific customer/procurement blocker; service-scope intake; board or management authorization to proceed; qualified EU legal-representative/designated-establishment provider or law-firm route; signed referral/resale or execution terms with that provider where available; appointment checklist; addressee contact and language requirements; internal request-intake owner list; data-category and system map limited to order-routing needs; emergency escalation table; conflict/escalation-to-counsel rule; customer-ready evidence letter; and dated proof of appointment, filed designation, provider acceptance, or qualified no-go. The startup controls the execution file, provider handoff, customer proof pack, and deadline tracker for that commercial blocker. It does not control law-enforcement authority decisions, order validity, production decisions, customer acceptance, or legal interpretation.

60-day signed/titled/assigned/prepaid proof:
Three EU legal-representative, privacy/compliance, or law-firm providers sign referral, resale, or execution routes for defined e-Evidence appointment cases. Eight digital service providers sign paid live-blocker mandates at 9,000-28,000 PLN each. Every accepted case has a named EU customer/procurement/legal/security/EU-launch request, management authorization, service-scope facts, and an agreed external appointment provider or counsel route. At least five cases produce signed appointment documents, provider acceptance, customer-ready proof, reseller/procurement progress, EU-launch checklist progress, or paid clean no-go because the provider is out of scope or requires counsel-led work. At least 90,000 PLN is collected within 60 days, and no case involves the startup receiving, interpreting, complying with, refusing, or transmitting actual law-enforcement orders.

6-month POC:
Deliver 35-70 live appointment-release cases; collect 450,000-1,400,000 PLN in case fees and partner-routed execution fees; document 25+ signed appointments, customer-proof acceptances, reseller/procurement progress outcomes, or qualified no-go decisions; build a private taxonomy of service-provider scope, customer request wording, appointment-provider requirements, language/contact obligations, internal escalation patterns, conflict-to-counsel triggers, buyer proof formats, and rejected cases; secure 6+ repeat routing partners among EU reps, privacy consultants, DPO firms, boutique law firms, security-questionnaire firms, and cross-border SaaS launch advisers; keep gross margin above 60% after external provider/referral costs.

First acquisition mechanism:
Target privacy consultants, DPO-as-a-service firms, EU representative providers, boutique tech/privacy law firms, security-questionnaire consultants, cross-border SaaS launch advisers, and cloud/hosting compliance operators. The pitch: "When a non-EU or cross-border digital service provider has an EU customer, reseller, or launch checklist blocked by e-Evidence designation proof, route the operational appointment file to me. I package scope facts, appointment-provider handoff, internal intake owners, customer-proof evidence, and no-go/counsel escalation. You keep legal advice and order-response work." Direct outreach goes to non-EU SaaS and infrastructure vendors with visible EU customers and no obvious EU legal/compliance operations.

Economics:
Charge 9,000-14,000 PLN for scope triage, appointment-provider routing, and customer-ready proof for simple providers; 16,000-28,000 PLN for multi-service SaaS, hosting, cloud, messaging, storage, or collaboration providers; and 30,000-55,000 PLN for urgent reseller/procurement/EU-launch blocks with multiple products or entities. External provider or counsel review is separately quoted or passed through. The provider pays because a 10,000-40,000 PLN execution file is cheap relative to a blocked EU enterprise renewal, reseller agreement, acquisition diligence item, or launch timeline.

Copy risk:
Law firms, privacy consultants, EU-representative providers, DPO firms, GRC consultants, security-questionnaire firms, and in-house counsel can copy pieces. The defense is not legal interpretation. It is partner-routed live blockers, fast service-scope triage, appointment-provider requirements memory, customer-proof wording, internal order-intake runbook templates, and a narrow case database of which providers and customer requests convert into signed appointments or clean no-go outcomes.

Why incumbents cannot copy before the founder controls the specific asset/account/case/claim/lot/payment stream:
Once the digital service provider signs the paid mandate, authorizes the scope file, chooses the external appointment route, and gives the buyer's written request and deadline, the startup controls the execution file, appointment-provider handoff, customer proof pack, and deadline response for that specific procurement or launch blocker. A law firm or EU-representative provider can compete generally, but it must displace an already-authorized live appointment file while the commercial deadline is running.

Why it is not a service, report, app, dashboard, database, marketplace, or generic broker:
The business refuses broad e-Evidence readiness reports, legal opinions, law-enforcement order handling, dashboards, marketplaces, generic compliance checklists, and introductions without a live blocker. It accepts only named customer/procurement/legal/security/EU-launch requests where the paid outcome is appointment execution, customer-ready proof, or qualified no-go. The deliverable is an appointment-release file: scope triage, external provider route, board/management authorization checklist, addressee/contact evidence, intake/escalation owner map, conflict-to-counsel rule, customer proof letter, and outcome record for the named commercial event.

Boundaries and legal/provider limits:
The startup does not provide legal advice, act as legal representative or designated establishment, receive European Production Orders or Preservation Orders, decide order validity, produce or preserve electronic evidence, advise on criminal procedure, interpret conflicts of law, provide DPA/GDPR opinions, negotiate customer contracts, or guarantee customer/procurement acceptance. Qualified EU legal representatives, designated establishments, law firms, DPOs, compliance officers, in-house counsel, and service-provider management remain responsible for appointment validity, legal interpretation, order handling, data protection, authority correspondence, and customer commitments. Any actual law-enforcement request, legal conflict, content-production issue, encryption dispute, third-country conflict, or privileged/sensitive matter is routed to qualified counsel and excluded from the desk.

Duplicate risk against existing confirmed ideas:
This is not DORA Bank Renewal Evidence Pack because it does not handle financial-sector ICT third-party-risk, DORA Article 28/30, subcontracting, audit rights, exit plans, operational resilience, or bank supplier questionnaires. It is not REDBlocked because it is not Amazon product compliance or connected-device evidence. It is not Data Act Machine Data Liberation because it does not handle connected-product data access or machine-data disputes. It is not AIQ, AIGate, RAGProbe, CyberBind, NIS2/UKSC, KSeF, CBAM, EUDR, MoCRA, CPSC eFile, GPSR, BioSignal, HeritageDoor, PromoLeak, BatteryFit, TraceFaktura, Supplement Stack, HeatQuiet, or any health/Rx/clinical route. The buyer trigger, control artifact, and proof are materially different: EU e-Evidence appointment execution and customer-ready addressee proof for digital service providers, not regulated-finance supplier evidence, product listing release, AI/security proof, or recovery claims.

Strongest anticipated objections:
Law firms and privacy consultants may own the trust relationship and treat this as normal compliance implementation. Many providers may be out of scope, already covered by EU counsel, or too small to pay. The startup cannot safely decide legal applicability, order validity, conflict-of-law issues, data-protection positions, or law-enforcement response. Customers may accept a counsel memo only, reducing the need for an operational desk. Appointment providers may not offer referral/resale terms to an unknown solo founder. The service-provider universe may be narrower than expected after excluding telecoms, mega-platforms, legal-order handling, and counsel-led cases. The August 2026 deadline may create a short spike rather than durable demand. Sensitive system, data-location, escalation, and authority-response information creates trust and security burdens. The workflow can become generic privacy/legal compliance coordination if live customer blockers and appointment proof are not enforced from day one.
