Score this business idea 0-100 and include strongest objections.

Founder context: unknown Warsaw solo founder, contractors allowed, max 100,000 PLN, 6-month POC, part-time.

Idea name:
ColoPower Reservation Assignment Desk

One-sentence thesis:
Option underused high-density European colocation rack and power reservations from tenants that no longer need them, then assign or novate those exact provider-approved kW/rack slots to AI, GPU, HPC, MSP, and inference buyers only after buyer deposits and provider non-objection.

Exact buyer:
Small and mid-sized AI infrastructure firms, GPU hosts, inference providers, MSPs, HPC users, sovereign-cloud builders, enterprise AI labs, and SaaS vendors that need near-term European colocation capacity with high-density power and cooling, but are too small for hyperscale deals and too urgent for multi-year data-center procurement. The supply side is a company with underused or unwanted contracted colocation capacity, expansion reservations, cage/rack commitments, high-density power allocations, or migration-delayed slots in European facilities where assignment, novation, or provider-approved sublease may be possible.

Acute trigger:
The buyer needs 20-100 kW of AI-ready rack/cage capacity within 30-120 days for GPUs, inference, HPC, sovereign hosting, customer deployment, migration, or expansion, but the provider's direct queue is slow, low-density, unavailable, or too large a commitment. The seller has a signed colocation order, expansion reservation, cage/rack commitment, or high-density power allocation that is idle, delayed, overcommitted, or no longer needed after a project cancellation, migration, cloud pivot, financing change, hardware delay, customer churn, or budget cut.

Control point:
Signed seller option or assignment mandate over a named colocation capacity block; provider name and facility; rack/cage count; committed kW and power density; cooling limits; start date and remaining term; monthly recurring charge and deposits; network/cross-connect constraints; technical fit notes; contract assignment, novation, sponsored sublease, or replacement-customer path; provider written non-objection or transfer-process confirmation; buyer deposit or escrow instruction; and closing terms that prevent any claim of control until provider approval is plausible. The desk controls a specific capacity transaction, not generic data-center leads.

60-day signed/titled/assigned/prepaid proof:
Within 60 days, sign two seller options or assignment mandates over named high-density colocation capacity blocks totaling at least 50 kW; obtain provider written non-objection, transfer-process confirmation, or sponsored-sublease path for at least one block; secure two buyer deposit or escrow instructions totaling at least 100,000 PLN equivalent for named capacity; close one assignment, novation, sponsored sublease, or replacement-customer transaction; and collect at least 50,000 PLN in assignment fee, option fee, success spread, or retained seller/buyer fee.

6-month POC:
Source 25-50 underused or cancellable high-density capacity blocks across Poland, Germany, Netherlands, Nordics, Baltics, Czechia, and nearby EU hubs; verify 8-15 as contractually and technically plausible; sign 5-10 seller options or assignment mandates; secure 5-10 buyer deposits or escrow instructions; close 3-6 provider-approved assignments, novations, sponsored subleases, or replacement-customer handoffs; generate 250,000-900,000 PLN gross revenue; keep legal/technical reviewer costs below 30% of gross revenue; and build a private map of provider transfer rules, density limits, cooling constraints, deposit terms, buyer profiles, rejected capacity reasons, and successful assignment language.

First acquisition mechanism:
Start on the supply side, not with generic buyer demand. Contact small GPU hosts, MSPs, crypto-to-AI operators, SaaS infrastructure teams, cloud migration consultants, insolvency advisers, hardware resellers, colocation brokers, CFOs of AI startups that downsized infrastructure plans, and companies that publicly announced AI/GPU or migration plans but changed direction. The seller pitch is: "If you have contracted high-density European colocation power you no longer need, I can option it for a named buyer subject to provider-approved assignment or novation; no public listing and no buyer exposure until the provider path is checked." The buyer pitch is only for named capacity: "This is a specific facility/kW/start-date slot with provider transfer path and refundable deposit terms, not a lead list."

Economics:
Charge sellers a small option/admin fee only where useful, charge buyers a 25,000-75,000 PLN refundable reservation deposit or escrow instruction for named capacity, and earn 3-8% of the first-year recurring colocation value, a 50,000-200,000 PLN assignment success fee, or a negotiated spread between seller exit value and buyer willingness to pay. The 100,000 PLN budget covers legal review, technical colocation reviewer time, seller sourcing, escrow setup, travel/calls, and small refundable deposits. The startup does not lease speculative capacity unless a buyer deposit and provider path already exist.

Copy risk:
Colocation providers, data-center brokers, cloud consultants, MSPs, AI infrastructure advisers, hardware resellers, and large tenants can copy pieces. The defense is early control of exact underused capacity blocks, signed seller options, provider transfer-path memory, buyer deposits, and facility-specific constraints before those slots are marketed broadly or reclaimed by the provider.

Why incumbents cannot copy before the founder controls the specific asset/account/case/claim/lot/payment stream:
Once the seller signs an option over a named rack/kW reservation, the buyer places a deposit for that exact block, and the provider confirms an assignment, novation, sponsored sublease, or replacement-customer process, another broker cannot sell that same capacity without displacing the signed parties or waiting for the option to expire. Competitors can source other capacity, but not the controlled facility, kW amount, term, start date, transfer path, and buyer deposit sequence.

Why it is not a service, report, app, dashboard, database, marketplace, or generic broker:
The business does not sell colocation consulting, cloud migration advice, lead lists, a data-center marketplace, dashboards, or generic introductions. It only works on named provider-approved high-density rack/kW capacity where a seller option, provider transfer path, and buyer deposit can be tied together. No named capacity block, no provider path, and no buyer deposit means no case.

Boundaries and legal/provider limits:
Do not sell electricity, operate a data center, provide cloud hosting, provide managed IT, guarantee uptime, guarantee GPU compatibility, provide engineering design, act as a regulated energy broker, bypass provider contract terms, or market capacity without seller authority and provider-approved transfer path. Use lawyers for assignment, novation, sublease, security deposit, non-circumvention, confidentiality, and liability terms. Use colocation/HPC technical reviewers for density, cooling, rack, cabling, cross-connect, carrier, redundancy, and power-fit checks. Reject capacity with non-transferable contracts, unclear seller authority, sanctions risk, unpaid provider balances, low-density ordinary racks, no provider approval path, vague verbal availability, speculative future builds, or buyer use cases that violate provider policy.

Duplicate risk against existing confirmed ideas:
This is distinct from Data Act Machine Data Liberation Desk because the controlled object is a provider-approved high-density colocation capacity assignment, not machine-data access. It is distinct from DORA, REDBlocked, BioSignal, CBAM, GreenTender, PromoLeak, HeritageDoor, TraceFaktura, BatteryFit, HeatQuiet, and AIQ/RAG evidence ideas because it does not involve regulatory evidence, marketplace tickets, diligence memos, payment recovery, physical heritage salvage, or questionnaire/security artifacts. It is distinct from CleanRIPE IPv4 options and GridSlot host-site options because it controls an existing contracted rack/kW reservation with provider transfer path and buyer deposit, not IP-address transfer brokerage or speculative grid-site origination.

Strongest anticipated objections:
Colocation providers may prohibit assignment, novation, or sublease, or may simply reclaim the slot and sell direct. Buyers may not deposit until the provider fully approves technical fit, credit, KYC, usage, term, and commercial terms. Underused high-density reservations may be rare because valuable capacity is already absorbed, cancelled, or known to brokers. Existing data-center brokers and providers have much stronger trust and buyer networks. Contracts may contain non-transfer, change-of-control, minimum-term, deposit, cross-connect, equipment, insurance, acceptable-use, and credit conditions that kill many apparent opportunities. Ordinary low-density rack space is not scarce enough. The seller may owe unpaid charges or have provider disputes. The buyer may need engineering design, hardware availability, network access, liquid cooling, remote hands, import logistics, or managed hosting beyond the assignment. The founder may look like an untrusted capacity broker unless provider non-objection and escrow discipline are real from the first case.
