## Strongest Objections

1. Many "undisputed" claims become disputed once pursued: defects, missing as-builts, unresolved snagging, warranty extensions, set-offs, backcharges, contractual penalties, or downstream unpaid obligations.
2. Small subcontractor document quality may be poor, forcing forensic reconstruction of protocols, ledgers, annexes, emails, handover records, and guarantee expiry dates.
3. Public and private retention claims behave differently; private contract wording varies and cannot be treated as one standard statutory workflow.
4. Retention money is legally nuanced, so templates and boundaries need counsel review.
5. Debtors may ignore a non-lawyer unless a lawyer sends the demand.
6. Success fees can feel expensive because the contractor believes the money is already owed.
7. Recovery cycles may exceed 60 days even when the claim is valid.
8. Channel partners may send bad cases, making the desk a dumping ground for disputes, insolvencies, missing documents, and angry subcontractors.
9. Contractors may avoid pressure because construction relationships are sensitive.
10. The model can drift into regulated or legal work unless it avoids legal advice, threats, litigation, claim buying, financing, and disputed defect analysis.

## Useful Lesson

Cash recovery alone is still insufficient when the "clean claim" boundary collapses in practice. Avoid construction retention variants unless a repeat channel supplies already acknowledged release schedules or beneficiary confirmations, not merely old contractual claims.
