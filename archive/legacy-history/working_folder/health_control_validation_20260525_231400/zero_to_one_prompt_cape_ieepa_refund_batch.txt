Score this business idea 0-100 and include strongest objections.

Founder context: unknown Warsaw solo founder, contractors allowed, max 100,000 PLN, 6-month POC, part-time.

Idea name:
CAPEClaim IEEPA Refund Batch Desk

One-sentence thesis:
Importers and smaller customs brokers facing the new CBP CAPE refund process for IEEPA duties need a broker-routed operations desk that turns ACE entry exports into CAPE-ready batches, accepted declaration status, refund-status ledgers, and reconciled cash without the startup filing customs claims or giving tariff legal advice.

Exact buyer:
Primary buyer: importers of record that paid IEEPA duties and have eligible or potentially eligible entries in ACE/CAPE Phase 1, especially CEE/EU brands, distributors, and manufacturers with U.S. importer-of-record exposure but thin internal U.S. customs operations. Channel buyer: small and mid-sized U.S. customs brokers whose importer clients have many CAPE-eligible entries but whose teams are overloaded by entry screening, ACH setup checks, CAPE batch preparation, status reconciliation, importer communication, and exception triage.

Acute trigger:
CBP opened CAPE Phase 1 in ACE for IEEPA refunds on April 20, 2026. Importers and authorized customs brokers need to identify eligible entries, confirm ACE/ACH refund setup, prepare CAPE declarations, handle accepted/rejected statuses, track liquidation/reliquidation and refund dates, and reconcile actual refund amounts. Missed eligible entries or rejected declarations delay cash.

Control point:
The startup only accepts a case after importer or broker authorization for named importer-of-record accounts and entry ranges; ACE/exported entry data supplied by the importer or authorized broker; prepaid batch fee; broker/IOR-approved eligibility screen and exclusion map; CAPE declaration worklist prepared for the licensed or authorized filer; status ledger for accepted, rejected, pending, liquidated/reliquidated, refund-issued, and exception entries; and success-fee terms tied only to accepted declarations or received refunds where attribution is explicit.

60-day signed/titled/assigned/prepaid proof:
Three signed importer or broker mandates; 500 entry lines screened; 150 entries passed to authorized broker/IOR as CAPE-ready batches; 75 accepted CAPE declarations or accepted/rejected status outcomes documented; at least one importer refund status or refund payment event traced; 60,000-180,000 PLN equivalent collected in prepaid batch fees and clearly attributed success fees; one licensed customs broker or trade-compliance reviewer retained for boundary review.

6-month POC:
Fifteen to thirty paid importer or broker mandates; 8,000-25,000 entry lines screened; 3,000-10,000 entries prepared for authorized CAPE filing; 1,500+ accepted/rejected/pending status outcomes tracked; 15+ refund-status or refund-payment traces; 300,000-900,000 PLN equivalent collected; at least three repeat broker or importer channels; gross margin above 60% after reviewer and data-cleanup costs; fewer than 25% of accepted batches requiring legal/customs interpretation beyond supplied broker guidance.

First acquisition mechanism:
Start with CEE/EU brands, distributors, and manufacturers that acted as U.S. importers of record during the IEEPA tariff period, plus small U.S. customs brokers serving importers with large entry counts but limited back-office capacity. The first outreach asks for one ACE export covering IEEPA-duty entries and offers a prepaid CAPE readiness screen and batch worklist that the importer or authorized broker files in ACE.

Economics:
2,000-5,000 USD importer setup and ACE/ACH/refund-readiness screen; 1-3 USD per entry screened for larger batches; 15-50 USD per CAPE-ready entry passed to the authorized filer depending on data quality; 2-6% success fee on refunds actually received only when the before-state entry list and after-state refund trace support attribution. The importer pays because refunds are direct cash, and late or rejected CAPE declarations delay money. Brokers pay or resell because entry cleanup, importer communication, ACH readiness, status tracking, and exception ledgers are operationally heavy but not strategic legal advice.

Copy risk:
Customs brokers, trade lawyers, Big4 customs teams, tariff-recovery specialists, and importer trade-compliance teams can copy the workflow. The defense is broker-routed batch execution, CEE/EU importer discovery, CAPE entry-screening workpapers, accepted/rejected status memory, and signed control over exact importer entry batches during a time-sensitive refund window.

Why incumbents cannot copy before the founder controls the specific asset/account/case/claim/lot/payment stream:
Once a broker or importer authorizes a named importer account and entry date range, supplies ACE exports, approves the worklist, and pays the batch fee, the exact refund-control object is locked: entry numbers, IOR authority, ACE/ACH readiness status, CAPE declaration worklist, acceptance/rejection history, and refund-status ledger. Competitors can copy the general CAPE workflow, but they cannot access or submit the same batch without the importer or broker switching mid-process.

Why it is not a service, report, app, dashboard, database, marketplace, or generic broker:
The product is a controlled refund-batch operation for named importer entries. The proof artifact is a CAPE-ready worklist filed by the authorized broker or IOR, accepted/rejected declaration status, liquidation/reliquidation/refund-status ledger, exception list, and reconciled refund cash. There is no marketplace, no software-first dashboard, no generic tariff memo, and no customs brokerage by the startup.

Boundaries and legal/provider limits:
The startup does not act as customs broker, file customs declarations unless the IOR or authorized broker does so in ACE, provide legal advice, provide tariff classification advice, decide eligibility where CBP/broker guidance is unclear, handle customs powers of attorney, route refunds to itself, take possession of refund funds, litigate, protest, or advise on surety/importer recipient disputes. The importer of record and authorized customs broker remain responsible for filings, eligibility decisions, ACE/ACH setup, refund recipient instructions, compliance review, and communications with CBP.

Duplicate risk against existing confirmed ideas:
This is not CBAM Import Continuity because it concerns U.S. IEEPA duty refunds through CAPE/ACE, not EU CBAM import exposure or broker-branded carbon-border continuity memos. It is not PromoLeak, Amazon Vendor Central recovery, retail deductions, 3PL overbilling, freight credits, KSeF payment release, PSE PV compensation, DORA, REDBlocked, BioSignal, HeritageDoor, Data Act, GreenTender, BatteryFit, HeatQuiet, or Supplement Stack Safety. The control point is a named importer/broker refund-entry batch and refund-status ledger.

Strongest anticipated objections:
Customs brokers and trade consultants may already handle CAPE filings for their clients. Many importers can follow CBP's public CAPE instructions without outside help. A Warsaw founder has weak U.S. customs credibility and no default access to ACE data. The opportunity may be time-windowed and shrink after the first refund wave. CAPE Phase 1 scope exclusions and entry status edge cases can require legal/customs interpretation. Sensitive importer, entry, banking, and refund data create trust friction. Success-fee attribution can be disputed if CBP would have issued the refund anyway. The business can become low-margin data cleanup unless entry volume, broker channels, and rejection rules are strict.
