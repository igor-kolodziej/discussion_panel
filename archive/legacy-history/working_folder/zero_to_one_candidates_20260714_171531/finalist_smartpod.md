# Zero-to-One Candidate: SmartPod

## One-Sentence Thesis

Own and circulate serialized spare sensor pods for employers using washable smart garments, contractually routing every removal, inspection, repair, firmware update, reassignment, and replacement through one cross-brand custody service.

## Founder Fit

The founder fits fleet analytics and process automation; a technician/logistics contractor handles batches. The business is weaker on access to compatible pod inventory and textile-service distribution.

## Customer, Pain, and Current Workaround

Employers using smart garments must remove electronics before washing, keep pods with workers/garments, inspect them, update firmware, repair failures, and avoid downtime. Current ownership is split among workers, laundry, IT, EHS, and the garment vendor.

## Product Wedge

A 50-garment pilot supplies a serialized spare-pod pool, scan stations, custody rules, batch inspection, replacement SLA, and a contract routing covered pod events. It is not a dashboard sold without inventory.

## Commercial Insight or Market Gap

Smart-workwear hardware can fail operationally at the wash/custody boundary. Recurring value would come from uptime and working capital, not from another physiological algorithm.

## Underused Momentum

Wearable electronics and smart garments are being deployed while textile-service infrastructure already manages ordinary workwear. The installed base of detachable compatible pods is not established.

## Beachhead Market

Employers already operating 50–500 detachable-pod smart garments and experiencing wash damage or pod loss. This named segment may be too small.

## Business Model and Unit Economics

10–25k PLN setup plus 45–75 PLN/garment/month. Expected 55 PLN; stressed model leaves only 4.6 PLN/garment/month contribution after pod depreciation, doubled CAC, support, and 30% higher costs.

## First 10 and 100 Customers

Public smart-workwear cases and employer outreach for the first ten; textile-service networks for 100. The first employer should contract directly, but the public list of compatible deployments is unproved.

## Primary Control Point

Owned serialized spare inventory plus a signed route for every covered pod event. Inventory is model-specific and ordinarily purchasable, making the control point copyable.

## Cold Start and Resource Bootstrap

Buy 20 pods and build custody workflow under 15k PLN; require a prepaid 50-garment pilot before more inventory. Access to several OEM pod models is an uncontrolled dependency.

## Distribution Advantage

Cross-brand fleet history could help, but OEMs/laundries already own customer access. No repeatable direct channel is demonstrated.

## Competitive Advantage and Expansion Path

Potential expansion from one garment to mixed wearable fleets, but RFID laundry/OEM platforms can add the process. Cross-brand firmware/inventory access is weak.

## Poland/EU or Local Adaptation Defense

European textile services provide infrastructure, not a local barrier. Poland may support logistics cheaply.

## Compounding Advantage

Failure data and installed inventory might compound only after scale; no first-year hard advantage prevents bundling.

## Founder Wealth Model

| Scenario | Volume/value | Revenue | Margin | Capital/ownership | Value/timing |
|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Conservative | 5k garments × 55/month | 3.3m PLN | 18% EBIT; 0.59m | 1m inventory; 85% | 4× × 85% = 2.0m year 7 |
| Expected claim | 30k × 55/month | 19.8m PLN | 23% EBIT; 4.55m | 5m inventory/ops; 75% | 4–6× × 75% = 13.6–20.5m year 8, but volume is heroic |
| Strong | 80k × 60/month | 57.6m PLN | 25% EBIT | 15m; 65% | 5× × 65% = 46.8m year 9 |

The grounded expected case fails the wealth gate. No retained cash is double counted.

## Reinvestment and Compounding Model

95% reinvestment and ≤5% distributions while employed in conservative/expected; strong 100%. Inventory consumes retained cash.

## Reachability and 180-Day Paid-Proof Plan

A 10–25k PLN prepaid 50-garment pilot is nominally possible under 38k PLN risk, but finding a compatible authorized employer among 100 accounts is unproved.

## Capital Tranches and Maximum Loss

15k PLN prototype inventory; ≤40k before paid pilot; ≤100k only after two employers and positive contribution; family capital excluded.

## Critical Dependency Map

Installed-base denominator, compatible spare access, employer authority, OEM firmware, laundry integration, and contribution after logistics.

## Full-Time Transition Gate

Three employer fleets, positive logistics contribution, repeatable acquisition, 12 months runway.

## Failure Recovery and Reusable Assets

Retain custody software, RFID process, failure data, and limited inventory. Do not pivot to generic fleet software or partnership-dependent laundry service.

## Critical Evidence

EU-OSHA and IPC show wearable/washed-electronics relevance; ETSA shows a textile-service sector. None proves the installed base.

## Critical Hypotheses

At least 100 directly reachable compatible fleets; downtime savings exceed 55 PLN/month; OEMs permit spare-pod pooling.

## Kill Criteria

Fewer than 100 named compatible fleets; no prepaid pilot in 180 days; OEM/laundry can add service routinely; stressed contribution below 10 PLN/garment/month; no durable control point by month 12.

## Budget, Time, and Minimum People Stack

Founder 10–15 hours/week; technician/logistics contractor. 15–38k PLN pre-proof.
