## Verdict

**Score: 65/100**

## Strongest objections and caveats

- **Founder Wealth Gate: fail.** The expected case derives founder wealth from 20,000 global annual units, 20% operating margin, and a 4–6× EBIT multiple. The proposal explains acquisition for roughly the first 100 customers, not a repeatable path to 20,000 annual buyers. Market size at this price, CAC, returns, subscription attachment/churn, and replacement demand remain unquantified; therefore this is plausible upside, not yet a non-heroic expected-success case.
- **Reachability Gate: conditional pass.** A prototype and 25 refundable preorders within 38,000 PLN are credible part-time proof targets. However, 25 preorders validate interest more than delivered economics: they cannot establish production yield, certification, warranty/support burden, return rates, small-batch fulfilment cost, or durable acquisition payback.
- Cross-game event classification is existential technical risk. Mixed audio, game updates, different sound assets, simultaneous events, audio processing settings, and strict latency requirements could make false cues too frequent or maintenance effectively game-specific.
- The price/value gap is unproven. Audio Radar already sells a no-integration directional-audio accessory for $149, while free native visual cues continue improving. Microsoft explicitly encourages games to provide redundant visual and haptic channels.
- Community participation, directories, and search are plausible first-100 channels but not proprietary distribution. Console, VR, navigation, and alarms are speculative adjacencies with materially different integration, regulatory, and buyer requirements.
- Founder fit is good for signal processing and experimentation but weaker for wearable manufacturing, accessibility co-design, global hardware support, and community-led distribution within 25 hours per week.

## Control point

**Tier 3.** The firmware, haptic vocabulary, device keys, and direct customer relationship are owned but not scarce; the supposed corpus, installed base, switching costs, and measured tactile-language advantage do not yet exist. Components and audio capture are commodity-accessible, the vocabulary is observable, and competitors can collect equivalent training data. This could earn Tier 2 after demonstrating a compounding installed base and hard-to-replicate performance data, but it is not Tier 1 merely because the company owns its product design and prototypes.

## Strongest copy risk

Audio Radar adding wrist-haptic endpoints. It already captures ordinary 7.1 game audio without studio integration, serves the same accessibility problem, sells worldwide at $149, claims patented technology, and has a Logitech partnership. It could reuse its existing directional-processing stack, audience, and distribution while adding haptic output, leaving CueBand's event classifier and tactile vocabulary as the main—and currently unproven—differentiators.
