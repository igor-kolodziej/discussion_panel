## Original Run Summary

The search explored **50 raw directions**, then added **two synthesized candidates** and **one structural pivot**. Ten directions became complete finalist briefs, and six exact canonicals received two independent simulated judges each. None passed every required gate.

The recurring failure patterns were:

- the startup owned a product but not a scarce control point;
- an incumbent already controlled buyer trust, workflow, data, certification, or distribution;
- the expected path to 5 million PLN of founder wealth required unsupported volume;
- a credible first sale was possible, but durable margins and repeatable acquisition were not;
- compliance, integration, support, or manufacturing exceeded the part-time capital envelope.

“Failed” below means rejected within this specific founder, capital, timing, and wealth framework—not necessarily that nobody could ever build the product.

## Wave One: Biosignals, Electrodes, and Reference Materials

1. **PouchSense** — A shelf-life witness placed inside wet-electrode packaging to predict hydrogel degradation. **Failed after panel:** electrode OEMs own the decisive aging and regulatory data and could internalize the indicator; the expected volume was unsupported. Official simulated score: 52.

2. **LotBridge** — Witness coupons for releasing plated electrode-coating lots. **Screened out:** it remained an easily internalized QA coupon, while the OEM or coating supplier controlled the real release data and workflow.

3. **SweatFlux** — Dynamic artificial-sweat cartridges for wearable-sensor regression testing. **Held as backup, then closed:** artificial sweat is readily available, the buyer universe is small, and no release-critical advantage over internal testing was established.

4. **TonePulse** — Calibrated optical skin films for PPG and pulse-oximeter development. **Screened out:** it repeated the ordinary phantom/reference-material pattern and did not prove that the film would change an engineering or regulatory decision.

5. **Interface Matrix** — Multimodal artificial-skin cards for testing several physiological sensors. **Screened out:** broad functionality weakened specialization, and incumbents or internal labs could reproduce the materials using ordinary suppliers.

6. **WashWitness** — Embedded swatches showing degradation in electronic textiles after washing. **Screened out:** standards and internal wash tests already cover much of the need; the swatch was easy to copy and the recurring buyer denominator was unclear.

7. **SweatSkin** — Reference sleeves for testing prosthetic liners under sweat and skin-like conditions. **Screened out:** it was another phantom variant with a small customer universe and no protected route into prosthetic-development decisions.

8. **MouthCouple** — Replaceable dentition cartridges simulating fit degradation in instrumented mouthguards. **Screened out:** it depended on dental-device trust and integration, had a narrow buyer base, and risked becoming custom testing work.

9. **DryPin** — A stocked component foundry for dry EEG, EMG, and EOG electrodes. **Held as backup, then closed:** freedom-to-operate, materials expertise, vertical integration by device makers, and commodity component pressure made the control point weak.

10. **ProbeRevive** — Recoating cartridges for worn neurophysiology probes. **Screened out:** a very small market, quality and liability exposure, and dependence on probe-maker specifications made it unsuitable for the wealth and reachability gates.

## Follow-Up Run: Distilled Brain/Biosignal Lessons

1. **Owning analysis is not owning the biosignal workflow.** A parser, acceptance check, archive container, or reconstruction script remains weak when a sponsor, OEM, site, QMS vendor, or platform controls raw data, protocol authority, and the release decision.
2. **Physiological data rights must be acquired by the paid wedge and survive real contracting.** Hoped-for derivative rights, revocable consent without attrition economics, or cross-client reuse that buyers may prohibit cannot support the wealth model.
3. **Wearable inventory is a control point only when it is genuinely scarce or contractually reserved.** Named lots and call-offs looked tangible, but existing suppliers already offered special stock, change notification, VMI, and small conversion.
4. **A filed patent or institute prototype is not controlled IP.** Before it counts, the option must be signed, the relevant territory and claims must exist, know-how access must be explicit, freedom to operate must be bounded, and a buyer must fund the next conformity step.
5. **Safety and clinical trust cannot be outsourced away in the spreadsheet.** Making the OEM or customer responsible for certification does not remove notified-body timing, liability, procurement, raw-access, and vendor-qualification dependencies from the first sale.
6. **Regulation supplies relevance, not automatic budget.** The MDR, Data Act, AI Act, Accessibility Act, PPE rules, and workplace-temperature duties did not mandate any proposed vendor or transaction.
7. **Human participant panels can solve cold-start data acquisition but often remain Tier 3.** Participants can withdraw or create equivalent recordings, buyers may reject retained rights, and established QA, biometric, or accessibility vendors can recruit similar cohorts.
8. **A reachable pilot still cannot compensate for a heroic account denominator.** PAR Rail, SkinLot, and MowaForge could all attempt bounded paid proof, yet their expected founder-wealth cases depended on unproved procurement capture, margins, or near-total penetration of a small buyer pool.
9. **Intelligent PPE and augmentation hardware are channel businesses before they are sensor businesses.** Certified incumbents already possess product families, distributor trust, service logistics, safety evidence, and installed buyers; superior sensing alone does not displace them.
10. **The strongest future-lab wedge must route an economically mandatory recurring event.** The desired shape is a paid workflow that repeatedly acquires usable physiological or behavioral data, hardware knowledge, or exclusive rights while providing measurable value to the same payer.

## Additional Takeaways From The 2026-07-15 Run

1. An advisory score above a threshold is not validation. HumanFactor Works scored 84 in advisory review and still failed its independent simulated gate at 74.
2. Acquiring distribution can create a real control point, but a roll-up wealth model must reconcile every acquisition, tax, debt-service, working-capital, replacement-operator, and integration cash flow—not only a terminal valuation.
3. A one-off paid diagnostic or proficiency test does not support subscription economics merely because the underlying risk recurs.
4. Synthetic human–AI workflow tests face a structural dilemma: isolation makes them cheap and comparable but may make them unlike production; integration makes them relevant but bespoke.
5. Moving from a test to live accepted outcomes does not help if the result is a professional exception-handling service inside a workflow already controlled by installed software vendors.
6. Future-lab asset creation must be intrinsic to paid delivery and the profitable core. An optional wearable or data module attached to an unrelated cash-flow business is not enough.

### Distilled Lessons From The 10:24 Run

1. A reachable paid experiment does not compensate for a non-conservative Founder Wealth path; several candidates passed Reachability but failed Wealth independently.
2. Legal ownership of equipment, files, or data is not automatically Tier-1 economic control. The business must control a durable, valuable event or scarce resource that buyers cannot route around cheaply.
3. Personalisation data compounds too slowly when an operating-system, platform, employer, or incumbent provider already owns distribution and the primary workflow.
4. End-of-life inventory creates urgency but rarely supports durable wealth unless the initial wedge itself establishes the later control point.
5. Test fixtures and benchmark products require either recognised authority, difficult-to-recreate predictive validity, or a mandatory release workflow; physical IP alone was readily copyable.
6. Returns and repair can create custody and failure data, but existing lifecycle operators begin with superior logistics trust, facilities, insurance, and procurement relationships.
7. The best official simulated score in this run was 72, below the required strict `>83`; real working-chat and fresh-chat validation were correctly not attempted, and no confirmed idea file was created.
